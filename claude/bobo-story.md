Here's a story. When I was at university, a wise professor once vented to me and
my fellow students during a lecture. This was for a course on algorithmic
theory; all to do with code specifications and invariants. It was an interesting
way to tackle coding, rather than relying on trial-and-error or attaching a
debugger to ensure algorithmic correctness. The course taught me how to think
critically and mathematically about code, rather than treating it like a machine
that can just be duct-taped together.

When the professor vented, he shared some of his professional experience. In a
non-academic—or should I say, commercial—environment, the programmers who get
the best reputations are the ones who write sloppy code very quickly. Their code
is usually buggy, and yet, everyone at the company loves them because they're so
fast and fix bugs right away.

Meanwhile, the professor, who took a more academic approach, was able to tackle
difficult problems by thinking them through, clearly understanding the problem,
and coming up with a simple solution that worked on the first try. Sadly, he was
deeply frowned upon by his coworkers; they felt it was very unfair that he was
always given the "easy tasks."
